So, what am I actually working on?

I'm on a team trying to teach a computer to do something every kid can do: pick up a game it has never seen, poke at it, and figure out how to win.

That's a real person on the right, playing one of the contest's games. Nobody tells you the rules. You press arrows, watch what changes, and work it out. People find this pretty easy. AI still finds it very hard.

The contest

Why games?

ARC Prize is a research contest built around one question: can an AI learn something genuinely new, the way a person does, instead of just repeating what it saw in training? The new version of the test uses little video games like the one above. Each one has its own hidden rules. The AI sees the screen, presses buttons, and has to figure out what's going on. It also gets graded on how few moves it wastes.

The chatbots most people use are amazing at questions that look like something they've seen before. Drop them into a situation with no instructions and they flounder. That gap is what this contest measures, and it's why I find it so interesting.

How I got here

It started with tools, and the people I met building them.

I'm a video game producer. More than a year and a half ago I got hooked on these puzzles, which are supposed to be easy for people and hard for AI. Some of them were hard for me too. I wanted to understand why.

So I built ARC Explainer, a site for looking at the puzzles and at how different AIs try to solve them. It grew into a resource for the whole ARC community, and ARC Prize's co-founder Mike Knoop thanked me by name for it in his year-end report. I also hosted weekly community chats, which is how I met Son Pham. We kept trading ideas about these games, big computers and AI coding tools until we were basically building something together.

Now I'm on the team itself, Team Turducken, making real entries instead of just building tools for other people's.

What we build

We don't build the brain. We build everything around it, and then we operate on it.

Nobody on our team trains an AI from scratch. That costs a fortune. We start with a model someone else built and released for free, and do three kinds of work on it.

  • The rig

    The software wrapped around the model, which people in the field call a harness. It turns the screen into something the model can read, gives it a notebook to keep track of what it has tried, and gives it a scratch pad where it can write little programs to test a hunch before spending a real move. Without this, even a smart model wanders around pressing buttons at random.

  • The coaching

    This is the part I didn't expect: a big share of the work is telling the model how to think. What to look at first. How to write down a guess about the rules. When to give up on a theory that keeps failing. What to remember between levels and what to throw away so it doesn't drown in its own notes.

    We test this obsessively. Small changes in wording swing the score more than you'd believe. We even tried having it think in a different language: our model was trained on a huge amount of Chinese, so we guessed thinking in Chinese might help. In our first test it did much worse. Nobody could have predicted that. You only find out by trying.

    This is where my game background pays off. I play these games myself and record it, so the model can learn from how a person actually works out the rules, not just from its own fumbling.

  • The surgery

    Son calls this part “lobotomizing the model.” It's more precise than it sounds. See below.

Son's lobotomy

The model we use is what's called a mixture of experts. Picture a huge committee of specialists. For every word it writes, only a handful of them speak up and the rest sit quiet. That makes it fast, but every member of the committee still has to fit into the computer's memory.

So we watch which specialists actually do the work while the model is playing well, and cut out the ones that hardly ever speak up. That's a big slice of the model gone. Then we give what's left a short round of extra training so the survivors learn to cover for the ones we removed. The result fits on the contest's machine with room to spare for its notes, and it runs fast enough to play more games in the time allowed.

It's a strange kind of engineering: taking a brain someone else built, removing parts of it, and teaching what remains to do the job anyway.

Open models

Why it all runs on a model you've probably never heard of.

You've heard of ChatGPT, maybe Claude or Gemini. Those live on their companies' computers. You rent them over the internet and you can't open them up.

This contest doesn't allow that. Your entry runs on the contest's own computer, cut off from the internet, with a fixed amount of memory and a time limit. So you need a model you can download and own outright. Those are called open models, and right now some of the best ones come from Chinese labs.

Rented

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini. Very capable. Can't be downloaded, changed or taken into the contest.

Owned

Qwen (made by Alibaba), DeepSeek, GLM and others. Free to download, run on your own hardware, and modify, which is exactly what we do.

Ours is built on Qwen. Like a lot of teams, we started from a good public setup another competitor shared and built on top of it. The other thing nobody tells you: a lot of the progress in this field doesn't come from us at all. A lab releases a better open model and everyone's plans change overnight. We keep a close eye on every release and try new ones the day they drop, because the next one could beat months of our own tinkering.

For you

What this means if you're just starting to use AI.

Most of what we've learned on the hard end applies to anyone typing into ChatGPT.

  1. The setup matters as much as the model. The same AI does dramatically better or worse depending on what you tell it and what you give it to work with. Give it context, an example, a clear goal.
  2. Tell it how to approach the problem, not just what you want. “Think through the options first, then pick one” or “ask me questions before you start” changes the result.
  3. Don't trust your instincts about what will work. Try it. We were sure a change would help and it backfired. Ask twice, ask differently, compare.
  4. Let it check its work. Our model gets a scratch pad to test ideas before committing. You can do the same thing by asking it to double-check or show its reasoning.
  5. Bigger isn't automatically better. We made our model smaller on purpose and it got more useful for our job. A quick, cheap model is often the right tool.
  6. Whatever you think AI can't do, check again in a few months. New models come out constantly. Something that failed last spring may be easy now.
