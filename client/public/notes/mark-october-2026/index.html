01 / How I got here

This started with curiosity, tools, and people.

ARC Explainer has been a long-running project for me. I’m a video game producer, and I got fascinated by puzzles that were supposed to be easy for people but difficult for AI. Some of them were damned difficult for me, too. I wanted to understand what was going on.

So I started building tools to make the puzzles and the AI’s answers easier to inspect. The original question was simple: if you give an AI the correct answer, can it explain why that answer is right? Even that turns out to be harder than it sounds.

Over time, ARC Explainer grew into a community resource: a place to browse puzzles, compare model attempts, read explanations, inspect results, and share useful tools. Later came playable ARC3 games, detailed game guides, and the ARC Daily Digest. There’s a lot of work behind the website you see now.

A thank-you that meant something.

In his December 5, 2025 year-end report, ARC Prize co-founder Mike Knoop publicly thanked me and Simon Strandgaard for our efforts to build tools, answer questions, and be a resource to the community. It was good to know the work was useful to other people.

I also hosted weekly ARC community chats. Through those conversations and the ARC Discord community, I met Son Pham. We both had a lot of enthusiasm for ARC3, big GPUs, and coding agents—the AI tools that help write and work on software. We kept talking, trying things, and helping each other. The collaboration grew organically.

This is my first time competing on a team from the inside. After all that time building resources, hosting conversations, and following other people’s work, I’m now part of the group making the submissions and trying to move up the board. That’s the big change.

02 / The actual problem

Can a computer learn the rules by playing?

You know how you can pick up an unfamiliar game, press a few buttons, notice what changes, and gradually work out what you’re supposed to do? That ordinary human ability turns out to be a serious challenge for AI.

ARC-AGI-3 tests it with unfamiliar visual games. The AI sees the screen and can take actions. It has to discover what the objects do, what counts as progress, and how to win—without a written explanation of the rules. The score also rewards using actions efficiently.

Imagine being handed a controller with no manual.

You move left. A blue square changes. Was that a switch, a wall, or a mistake? You try something else, compare the result, and revise your idea. That loop—observe, guess, test, remember—is what we’re trying to make work reliably.

This matters beyond games: an AI that can handle a familiar question is useful; an AI that can learn its way through an unfamiliar situation is a much harder engineering problem. These games give us a way to test that ability.

03 / What we’re building

We call it the flying turducken.

Our ARC3 team is called Team Turducken. A turducken is a chicken inside a duck inside a turkey; in our artwork, the whole contraption has engines and flies. It’s an appropriately absurd mascot for a system assembled from a lot of interacting parts.

I’m working with Son Pham and the others on the software around the AI: how it sees a game, keeps track of what happened, chooses its next move, and learns from failure. Using an existing AI model is only part of it. How we organize its play can make a huge difference.

My video-game background fits naturally into this. I can play the games, examine what makes their rules understandable, write down how a person works them out, and help build useful practice games. We’re also developing training material from successful play and human reasoning.

The community tools are still part of what I do. The difference now is that the people I met through that work have become collaborators and teammates.

The hard part is getting better at games the system hasn’t seen. A change that improves a practice game is a promising idea; the competition has to tell us whether it really helped.

04 / Where we stand

Third is real. The race is still running.

At the time of this update, Team Turducken is third out of 4,556 teams on the public ARC3 leaderboard, behind Tufa Labs and Yi-Chia Chen.

That’s the current public ranking, not a final result or a medal already won. The final standings depend on performance on the hidden competition games. So there’s plenty left to do—and plenty of room for the order to change.

For me, the exciting part is sharing responsibility for an actual competition entry with people I got to know through the community. We’re building, testing, submitting, and finding out what works together.

05 / Next stop

Boston, this Friday.

We’re looking forward to going to the ARC Prize Research Summit in Boston on Friday, October 23. The ARC Prize Foundation is hosting it in collaboration with MIT.

It brings together researchers working on how AI learns, reasons, and discovers things. For me, it brings together the community side and the competition side: meeting people, talking through what we’ve been building, and hearing what others are finding.

So that’s what’s new: third place, a trip to Boston, and a flying turducken held together by software, experiments, and an unreasonable amount of cybernetic poultry.
