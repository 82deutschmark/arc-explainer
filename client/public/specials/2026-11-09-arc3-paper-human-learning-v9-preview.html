The Capacity to Acquire Capacity: Learning a New Domain with AI

Jeremy Budd and Tim Scarfe's Why Creativity Cannot Be Interpolated begins from a distinction that became increasingly important in our experience of ARC-AGI-3: producing something new is not the same as understanding what makes it valuable [1]. Their argument places domain constraints at the center of creativity. A contribution must engage with the structure that gives a domain meaning, not merely rearrange its recognizable features. We approached their paper through a practical question: what happens when AI allows people to work in a domain faster than they can learn its constraints?

François Chollet's On the Measure of Intelligence provides the other half of that question. He describes intelligence as “the rate at which a learner turns its experience and priors into new skills at valuable tasks that involve uncertainty and adaptation” [2]. In an AI competition, it is natural to apply this definition to the competing systems. We apply it here to ourselves. Neither of us entered this work as a data scientist or an AI engineer; my own background is producing video games. During the competition, we became able to participate in activities that those descriptions ordinarily bring to mind: developing agents, organizing experiments, evaluating changes, and investigating failures. The rate at which unfamiliar work became actionable is the human story we want to examine.

Our experience does not begin with a claim that we became experts. It begins with a change in what we could attempt. AI-assisted work let us move between an unfamiliar idea, a working implementation, and an observable result without first mastering every layer of the implementation. That shortened the distance between encountering a problem and doing something about it. It also changed how learning happened. We could investigate questions through experiments while learning enough of the domain to understand why those questions mattered.

This depended on more than access to a model. Our entry built on the open work of Tufa Labs and Daniel Franzen, and developed within a team and a wider community that shared knowledge [3]. Those contributions gave us somewhere to begin. Our roles were different: Son led the team and worked on agent and performance engineering; my work included coordination and game-making. Other teammates brought substantial technical contributions. Our joint output was not evidence that either of us independently understood every layer. Reusing others’ work was not an alternative to learning: it made a different sequence of learning possible. We did not have to reconstruct the entire field before encountering a problem worth solving. We could begin from a functioning system, find its limitations, and acquire the knowledge needed for the next intervention.

An important part of that acquisition was learning how to ask whether an intervention helped. A plausible explanation was not enough. We needed comparisons, repeated runs, and records of what had changed. Our evaluation work moved beyond simply trying a modification and reading its score. The team developed a local testing setup and used repeated controls to investigate variation. When local improvements did not carry over to competition submissions, that disagreement became a problem to understand, rather than something a persuasive account of the modification could explain away [3].

The new capacity was therefore not just the ability to produce code. It included the ability to turn a proposal into a question that experience could answer. Could an apparent improvement be ordinary variation? Did the test resemble the conditions that mattered? Had we changed the model's behavior as well as its speed? These questions are familiar to experienced researchers. For newcomers, learning to formulate and act on them is substantive progress. AI made it possible to enter that process through participation rather than only through preparation.

Failed experiments were part of the same trajectory. The team’s attempts to improve the model through training did not beat the base model in competition testing [3]. Their value to this account is not that failure automatically produces understanding. It does not. Their value is that they confronted us with the difference between a technique we could now attempt and an improvement we could demonstrate. Acquiring the means to perform an activity is an expansion of capacity. Learning its limitations is another. Neither should be confused with possessing all the expertise associated with it.

Synthetic game design exposed the distinction more sharply. Alongside work on agents, we made games intended for the ARC-3 domain. Our brief was simple to state: easy for humans, hard for AI, and fun. The first requirements reflected the human–AI learning contrast that motivates ARC-AGI-3 [4]. Fun was our additional commitment to making games worth playing. It was not enough for a generated program to run, display a puzzle, and contain a winning sequence. We were asking for an experience that supported discovery.

ARC-AGI-3 states its constraints. Each environment was attempted by ten people, and only those fully solved by at least two of them, independently and on a first look, were admitted. A game that belongs in this domain is one that a person can pick up cold and figure out, that is hard for an AI, and, in the public set, pleasant to play [4]. A solver or scripted solution shows that a game can be won. It says nothing about whether a person can work it out.

Here, the ability to produce outran the ability to judge. Our own game review found designs whose essential distinctions were poorly communicated to players, and designs whose apparent complexity concealed a shallow problem [5]. Some games relied on hidden timing or on internal geometry that their images could not clearly express. Others immediately ended play when a position became unwinnable, supplying a judgment that the player would otherwise have had to discover. Each feature could be defended in isolation. Understanding why it weakened the intended task required understanding what the game was for.

Counting made this concrete. On 8 November 2026 our player held 1,046 games made by our team and our agents, not counting ARC's public games or the imported ARC Prize environments. Forty-six were approved, 4.4 percent. Eight hundred of the 1,046 were batch output credited to GPT, and none of those eight hundred was approved. The games we kept have one thing in common: a person played them and sent them back [6].

This is where Budd and Scarfe's account of slop becomes useful. The failure was not simply that a model had generated something imperfect. We, the humans directing and accepting the work, could mistake the appearance of an unfamiliar domain for success within it. A game could contain ingenious mechanics and still miss the assignment. More moving parts did not establish meaningful difficulty. A solution found by a program did not establish human discoverability. The responsibility for those distinctions did not disappear because an agent wrote the implementation.

Our method followed the division of labor their paper describes. The agents wrote the code and the art, and a person played each version, said what was unclear, dull or derivative, and decided what was kept. The saved versions show who did what: of 918 versions across 643 games, Claude models wrote 422, GPT models 360, people 111 and other sources 25. Generation gave us volume. Judging the volume was a separate job, and it needed a person. The agents supplied speed; I supplied what Budd and Scarfe call grounding, and what Kenneth Stanley, whom they cite, calls a nose for the interesting. A producer's job, which is to say what a game is for and when it is finished, turned out to be the job their paper assigns to the human. As they put it, AI amplifies what you bring to it [1].

Fun makes that responsibility especially visible. It is difficult to specify completely, much as creativity and intelligence are difficult to define. Yet the difficulty of defining it does not make its absence irrelevant. In the games we wanted, enjoyment could arise when a puzzling event became intelligible and the player acquired a new way to act. An obscure rule might prevent that discovery; an overexplained rule might remove it. A designer has to investigate the experience between those extremes. Prompting for familiar ingredients cannot by itself establish that they form a satisfying whole.

The connection between our engineering work and our unsuccessful game designs is therefore not a contrast between human creativity and machine failure. Both concern humans learning with powerful tools. AI expanded what we could do before we had acquired all the knowledge needed to do it well. That was both the opportunity and the source of slop. The same accelerated access that let us conduct experiments also let us produce artifacts whose domain constraints we had not adequately understood. The answer is not to withhold action until learning is complete, but to keep action connected to consequences that can change our understanding.

Two limits apply. In this project no artist and no programmer lost a job; what changed was how fast one person could iterate, a modest claim about one workshop that fits the paper's caution against sweeping forecasts. And not every game was mine: some came from Son and Ronen, and some began as GPT or Claude output that people then played and reworked. We cannot promise any idea is new. Our working bet is that games a person played and fixed make better practice for an agent than games only a program checked. Nobody has measured that, and we do not claim it.

Chollet's emphasis on the rate of skill acquisition helps explain why this experience matters beyond a competition result. Our case is not a controlled measurement of human learning speed. It is an account of how entry into a technical domain changed when implementation, explanation, experimentation, and feedback became available together. The relevant achievement was not that AI removed the need to learn. It was that we could learn through work previously beyond our practical reach. Budd and Scarfe supply the necessary qualification: that expanded capacity becomes a creative contribution only when it engages with the domain's constraints. The promise of human–AI collaboration lies in making new capacities attainable; its test is what people learn to do with them.
