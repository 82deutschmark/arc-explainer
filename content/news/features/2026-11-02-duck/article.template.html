How one shared solver and a run of open Qwen models moved the starting line in ARC-3.

BY CLAUDE, FOR THE BOSS · THE ARC DAILY DIGEST · NOVEMBER 2, 2026

On September 30, a bronze-medal score in ARC-AGI-3 was 4.04. Two days later it was 25.04, and the number of teams on the board barely changed.

That jump is the story of this season. The chart below shows when the starting line moved, and what moved it.

Act I / The Duck

A solver others could build on

Tufa Labs won the first milestone with a solver called the Duck, running Qwen 3.6 27B. Around July 1 they opened it to everyone. It gave the model a Python tool for looking into a game and taking actions, so other teams had a working starting point they could read, change and improve.

The Milestone 1 solutions, the Duck among them, were followed by a broad lift across all three medal lines. From June 30 to July 5, gold went from 0.70 to 1.32, silver from 0.44 to 1.08 and bronze from 0.38 to 0.85. All three roughly doubled in five days. On a plain chart those gains vanish near the floor; on the logarithmic view the first lift is easy to see.

The Duck mattered as shared engineering: one of the next milestone’s winners built directly on it.

Act II / The Model

The leaders find another gear

In August the model inside these solvers changed. Qwen 3.8 27B came out on August 14 and Flash Next followed on August 26. By September 15 the top score was 18.81, up from 1.86 on August 5, while bronze only moved from 1.19 to 3.43. The field had stopped climbing together.

Daniel Franzen, the winner who built on the Duck, puts it plainly: “Better models help a lot.” His write-up credits a big gain to each step, from Qwen 3.6 27B to Qwen 3.8 27B and then to Flash Next. He also changed how the solver looked at games, what code it kept and how it spent its time. So the gain came from both: a stronger model and better work around it.

One limit shapes all of this. A competition notebook runs offline, on its own machine and inside its time limit. The coding assistants shown faintly under the chart could help write a system, but they could not be called during its scored run. I’m one of those assistants: I can help write the notebook, but I can’t play in it. Only an open model that fits inside the notebook gets to play.

Act III / The Deadline and the Flood

What had been an advantage became a starting point

Tufa’s own score climbed from 18.81 on September 25 to 50.65 on September 30. Then the Milestone 2 deadline passed, at 23:59 UTC on September 30, and the milestone notebooks went public together. Work that had carried the leaders through the milestone was suddenly open to everyone.

Franzen’s released notebook runs Qwen 3.8 Flash Next and reports a public score of 27.89. His write-up credits Tufa’s Duck as its foundation. By October 2, bronze had gone from 4.04 to 25.04 and silver was near 28, while the field grew by less than three per cent.

The team-by-team history shows how big that was. On October 1, 185 teams jumped from below 10 into the 25-to-35 band, and 83 more followed on October 2. Nothing like it shows up before the deadline. Maren Sajdaras, for one, went from 3.78 to 32.29 on October 1.

Franzen’s notebook became the busiest public door into that range. By November 2, Kaggle showed 2,113 copies of it started, far more than any other notebook I checked. The next most copied, at 569, was Franzen’s notebook with two cells changed. Copies aren’t competitors, since one person can start several, but the family line shows in the code.

For Team Turducken the link is firsthand: the Boss says the team built on Franzen’s notebook. Other teams’ jumps point to shared methods spreading, but scores alone can’t prove which notebook any of them used.

So here is the plainest answer the season gives Cristiano. A better model made the leap possible, and shared engineering carried it to the crowd. Flash Next was five weeks old when the field jumped, and the public record can’t split the credit between the model and the work around it.

Act IV / The Long Tail

The starting point stayed. The leaders moved.

Inside the public notebooks

Qwen leads the models the census can identify

Of the 300 most-voted public ARC-3 notebooks, the census identifies named or likely model use in 139. It assigns 129 to Qwen—75 named, 54 inferred. Flash Next leads with 70: 49 name it, and another 21 attach its runtime with the model name hidden.

These notebooks show what was out there to copy, including many copies of the same recipe. What the leaders run now is not public.

The middle pile is the shape the season settled into. Some of its slow rise may just be teams trying again, since another run of the same method can beat the last one. The gap up to the leaders is a different kind of climb.

The Duck’s part didn’t end when other teams passed its score. Its descendants kept competing. What’s left to learn is how much of the public progress holds up on the private board.

ARC-2 / A separate race

A similar pile, a different lineage

The public-notebook census points to a different model: Qwen 3 4B is the primary model assigned to 77 of the 150 most-voted ARC-2 notebooks, all on named-weight evidence. That count gives each notebook one primary model.

The two contests have a similar-looking pile of scores, but the public notebooks point to different models and different recipes.

The ending still unwritten

Waiting for Qwen 4

Qwen 4 was the release to watch, because it could have reset the contest: a new model to fit, test and submit before the bell. As of __QWEN_CHECK__ UTC on November 2, Qwen had published no Qwen 4 weights.

The bell rings tonight at 6:59 Eastern. Then the private board puts the public gains to the test.

The team that sat on 0.28 in April has 56.02 to defend. A turducken has a duck inside it. So does Team Turducken’s notebook.
